01 — The Product

A shared ecosystem for everyone involved in learning to drive.

DriveFlow connects driving schools, instructors and students inside a single digital ecosystem, with platform-level admin operations running underneath all three. It's built for the Danish driving-licence training market.

A driving school creates its organization and manages its operational information — instructors, students, training offerings, and how lessons and packages are structured. Schools build training packages tied to specific driving-licence categories, combining theory and practical lessons.

Students join a school, explore the training options available to them, purchase what they need, and move through their learning journey. Instructors maintain their own accounts, declare which licence categories they're qualified to teach, and coordinate practical lessons directly with students — availability, date, time and meeting location.

Admin and platform operations sit around all three roles, holding the structure together. It's this web of interdependent roles — not a single user doing a single task — that made DriveFlow a genuinely different kind of design problem from a typical consumer app.

02 — The Challenge

Four roles, four sets of goals — one connected set of workflows.

Every role in DriveFlow has different responsibilities and different permissions, but none of them operate in isolation. A decision made by one role reshapes what's possible for the next. The design challenge was to give each role a workflow that felt like its own, while keeping the handoffs between them coherent.

03 — My Role

I was the bridge between product requirements, user experience and implementation.

I was the Product Designer on DriveFlow from early-stage discovery through delivery — not designing isolated screens, but sitting at the center of the collaboration between the client, design, frontend, backend and QA, for the length of the engagement.

Upstream

Client & Requirements

Direct client and stakeholder communication, clarifying requirements, and product/UX research where it was needed.

Center

Product Design

Flows, UI, reusable components and consistency — designed from the ground up and refined as the product evolved.

Downstream

Engineering & QA

Working with frontend and backend on constraints and dependencies, reviewing implementation, and testing features myself.

"Understanding requirements, translating them into flows, and staying involved until they were correctly built."
  • Communicating directly with the client and stakeholders
  • Understanding and clarifying product requirements
  • Conducting product/UX research where required
  • Translating requirements into user flows and product experiences
  • Designing product flows from the ground up
  • Designing the UI and establishing consistency across the product
  • Creating reusable components and interaction patterns
  • Working closely with frontend and backend developers
  • Discussing technical constraints and dependencies
  • Reviewing implemented functionality with the QA team
  • Testing developed features myself
  • Investigating design/implementation inconsistencies
  • Proposing solutions when requirements or constraints changed
  • Validating solutions with the client and supporting delivery
04 — Understanding the Users

Four roles, four different jobs to be done.

Role 01

Driving School

Sets up and runs the organization: operational information, instructors, students, and the training offerings and lesson/package structures the school makes available.

Role 02

Instructor

Maintains their own account and declares the driving-licence categories they're qualified to teach, then works directly with students to coordinate practical lessons.

Role 03

Student

Joins a driving school, explores available training and package options, purchases what they need, and progresses through their learning journey toward the test.

Role 04

Admin

Operates the platform layer that the other three roles run on — the operational backbone supporting schools, instructors and students across the ecosystem.

05 — From Requirements to Flows

Designed flow-by-flow, not screen-by-screen.

With four interdependent roles, a screen rarely stood alone — it was one step in a flow that touched other roles' data and permissions. Each requirement had to be worked through as a full journey before any UI was drawn.

01

Understand the requirement

Start from what the stakeholder actually needs, not the first proposed solution.

02

Map dependencies

Identify which other roles, permissions or data this flow touches before designing it.

03

Define the user journey

Lay out the path from entry point to completion for the role in question.

04

Identify edge cases

Work through what happens when data is missing, permissions differ, or a step is skipped.

05

Design interaction states

Cover the states a flow can actually be in — not just its happy path.

06

Validate with stakeholders

Check the flow against the requirement before it moves toward implementation.

07

Prepare for implementation

Hand the flow to development in a state that anticipates the technical questions.

08 — Component System

A reusable component system, built for four roles at once.

With multiple roles interacting across a large number of connected workflows, consistency wasn't a nicety — it was what kept the product legible. Buttons, inputs, cards, tables and status indicators were built once and reused across every role's surface.

12 — Outcome

A connected foundation, not a fixed set of screens.

Multi-role ecosystem
A connected experience spanning schools, instructors, students and admin, rather than four disconnected apps.
Structured workflows
Flows that carry a requirement from one role's action through to the next role's response.
Reusable patterns
A component system that let new flows be assembled from established, consistent parts.
Cross-team collaboration
A working rhythm between product, engineering and QA that held up as the product grew.
Continuous validation
Design decisions checked against both stakeholder intent and technical reality, repeatedly.
A scalable foundation
A base built to accommodate the different needs of driving-school stakeholders going forward.

13 — Key Learnings

What this engagement reinforced.

01

Designing an interconnected ecosystem means every screen is a step in someone else's flow, too.

02

Complexity across multiple user roles has to be managed at the flow level, before it reaches the UI.

03

Ambiguous stakeholder requirements become usable product only once they're mapped as journeys.

04

Good design decisions hold up because they're made within real engineering constraints, not around them.

05

Design isn't a one-time handoff on a product like this — it's validated through implementation, continuously.
